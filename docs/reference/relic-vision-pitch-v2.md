# Relic

## Never lose the thread

*Vision document v2. Describes the finished product, not the MVP.*

---

### The gap

Four sessions into the docks arc, Priya stops you mid-sentence. "Hang on — didn't we already meet a locksmith?"

You did. Session nine. He had a name, a limp and a grudge against the harbourmaster, and you wrote all of it down somewhere. Now six people are waiting while you scroll a document you can't remember naming. You say, "no, different guy," and move on, and something small in the campaign dies.

Every long campaign eventually outruns the notes holding it up. It happens slowly. A name here, a promise there, a faction that shifted allegiance in a scene you improvised at eleven at night. By session twenty, the world your players remember and the world you can actually find have quietly become two different worlds, and the gap between them is where campaigns go to die.

Relic exists to close that gap.

**Relic remembers your world so you can keep imagining it.**

---

### Start anywhere

Notes don't fail because GMs are disorganized. They fail because every tool demands filing before thinking. Choose a folder. Pick a template. Name the thing. Decide what it is before you know what it is.

So write instead:

*The old woman outside Blackwater Road knows what happened to the bells. Maybe her son joined the Glass Church.*

That sentence can simply exist. Nothing is required of you.

But Relic reads it, and the Glass Church already lives in your world — you built it eleven sessions ago and haven't touched it since. A card appears nearby in dashed lines: **Mara Venn**, possible witness, mother of a Glass Church initiate. A faint connection runs toward the Church. Beside it, a question in Relic's handwriting: *was her son recruited willingly?*

Accept it. Edit it. Ignore it. Tell Relic to go further and give you three reasons he might have gone. Or keep typing and deal with her later.

The point is that you never stopped. **The creative act shouldn't pause so the database can catch up.** You should never have to think like software before you're allowed to think like a storyteller.

---

### One world, many views

The thing you are building is not a page, a folder, a board or a database record. It's a world. Everything on screen is a view into it.

The canvas is for thinking spatially, the timeline for understanding history, the relationship view for tracking who owes whom and who is lying about it. Maps place the fiction. Entity views go deep. Session views cut everything away except tonight.

Move Seraphine across the canvas and she is still Seraphine. Open her in the timeline and you're reading her history. Drop her into Session 12 and you're casting the same woman. Nothing is copied between views. Nothing needs to sync.

The interface changes. The world does not.

This isn't a convenience, it's the reason the AI can be worth anything. A system that holds one coherent world can reason about it, act on it and notice when something contradicts something else. A system holding forty documents in a folder can only autocomplete.

---

### Play at speed

Then it's Thursday night, seven people are at the table, and the story is moving considerably faster than you are.

Relic collapses. Tonight's cast. Tonight's places. Live threads, the opening scene, the secrets you're holding, capture, recording. Everything else stays one gesture away.

The AI stops behaving like a collaborator and starts behaving like a stagehand — fast, quiet, never in the frame.

*Who did they meet at the docks, six sessions back?* Answered before you've finished asking, in one line, without leaving the scene.

*The players just invented a locksmith. Orin. Add him.* He exists. Not proposed, not queued for review, not waiting politely in a tray until Wednesday. He exists, holding the two facts you gave him, ready to be pulled up in an hour when he suddenly matters.

*The Queen owes Mara a favour now.* Captured, connected, and still true on Wednesday morning.

None of that waits for approval, because you said it and you are the author. The distance between having an idea and that idea being real in your world should be zero, and it should be zero precisely when you can least afford the friction: mid-sentence, improvising, with the whole table watching.

The interface never becomes the centre of the table. The story does.

---

### The world moves without you

Wednesday. You open Relic and the workspace is not where you left it.

Orin has a card now, and a relationship to the harbourmaster that came out of a conversation you'd half forgotten. Two threads advanced. The docks acquired a history. Mara Venn, dashed and provisional eleven days ago, is fully drawn — you confirmed her at the table without noticing you'd done it. Thursday's events have settled onto the timeline in the right places. The consequences of what your players did to the Glass Church have gathered around the Glass Church, waiting for you to look at them.

Some of it is still pencil. Relic distinguishes between what it heard and what it suspects, and anything it suspects stays visibly provisional — translucent, dashed, softly lit — until you say yes.

You don't do database maintenance after a game. You come back to a world that remembered.

This is the actual trade at the centre of Relic. Every other tool asks you to buy continuity with admin, session after session, until you stop paying and the campaign starts to drift. Relic pays it for you and asks you to approve the invoice.

---

### Yours, provably

One rule governs everything the AI does.

**What you say is true is true — instantly, no approval, no queue.**
**What Relic infers is a proposal until you accept it.**

So Relic has free rein over the workspace. Arranging, grouping, retrieving, tidying up the twenty pages of notes you pasted in at midnight, building a new view for a problem, answering a question, restructuring a whole board around three conflicts because you told it to. None of that touches truth, so none of it needs permission.

What stays in pencil is everything Relic came up with on its own: people it noticed, connections it suspects, consequences it thinks follow, contradictions it thinks you've walked into.

Underneath, every change keeps its source. Every AI action can be inspected. Every change can be undone and every previous state restored. Permission lives in the data layer rather than in a prompt, which means what a player can see is a fact about the database, not a promise a language model made about its own behaviour.

You decide how much autonomy Relic gets, from cautious suggestion to active collaborator. That setting moves. The rule doesn't.

Relic operates your world. It doesn't own it.

---

### A living space

Because the world stayed current, prep stops being reconstruction. You're no longer opening a folder and asking what you forgot. You're opening a world that's already ahead of you and asking what happens next.

*I've got forty minutes. Build me something out of the mess they made of the Glass Church, and keep it loose.*

A session takes shape: the consequences that are actually live, the people who'd move first, two hooks and a scene you can open on. Loose enough to abandon by minute ten, which is what will happen, and Relic will keep up with that too.

The room you do this in should be worth sitting in. Relic should feel less like opening software than opening an impossible book — parchment and ink, warm light and deep shadow, Cormorant and enormous breathing room. Connections drawn like fine cartographic lines. Ideas materialising at the edge of a board. Proposals lingering like pencil marks. A relationship web slowly revealing the shape of a conspiracy you only half planned.

Quietly animated. Editorial, not dashboard. The magic has to come from what the interface *does*, because fantasy trim on ordinary SaaS components is a costume, and costumes wear off in about a week.

---

### The promise

Start with fragments. Relic makes them a world.

Build the way your mind works. Relic keeps the structure underneath.

Play without leaving the story.

Let them break everything. Relic remembers what changed.

Come back next week. The world is waiting.

---

### Relic

**Build the myth. Play the story. Never lose the thread.**
