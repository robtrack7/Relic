# Relic — How it works

## Interaction Vision v3

*Companion to the Relic vision pitch. Describes the finished product’s interaction model, not the MVP.*

---

## The premise

Relic is not a collection of documents about a fictional world.

It is the place where that world exists.

Characters, places, relationships, fragments, histories, rumours, sessions and unanswered questions all belong to one persistent reality. Everything on screen is simply a way of looking at or acting upon it.

The canvas arranges it.

The timeline reveals its history.

The map reveals its geography.

The relationship view reveals its tensions.

The Stage narrows it to tonight.

The Librarian helps you understand and operate it.

**The interface changes. The world does not.**

---

# 1. Thought before structure

Worldbuilding begins with fragments:

*Maybe Mara knows why.*

*A bitter smell in the chapel.*

*Three bells missing.*

*What if the cult is right?*

Relic lets these exist immediately.

Write. Paste. Speak. Drop in an image. Scatter notes across a canvas.

Nothing needs a type, template, folder or title.

Fragments may later become Characters, Places, Events, relationships or something else. They may also remain fragments forever.

Relic can discover structure without demanding it from the GM.

**The creative act comes first. The system catches up.**

---

# 2. One world, many representations

Mara Venn is not a card or a page.

Those are representations of Mara.

Mention her in prose and her name resolves to the same person. Place her on a Board and she appears there. Focus on her and more becomes visible. Move to the timeline and you see her history. Pull her into tonight’s session and you see what matters now.

There is no *convert note into character*.

There is only increasing resolution.

A thing may appear as a word, a card, a focused object, a deep view or part of a larger pattern.

**You approach the world rather than navigate between files about it.**

---

# 3. Views are compositions

The Desk is what you are currently thinking about.

A Board is a remembered composition of the world:

*The docks.*

*The succession crisis.*

*Everything connected to the bells.*

Neither contains anything.

A Board remembers which parts of the world were visible, where they were placed and how they related to the problem you were exploring.

The same Character can appear on ten Boards without duplication.

Nothing gets filed.

Nothing belongs to one view.

Delete the Board and Mara still exists.

**The canvas is a view. The canvas is not the database.**

This also gives the Librarian freedom to reshape a composition without rewriting reality:

*Show me this conflict from the Glass Church’s point of view.*

The view changes.

The world does not.

---

# 4. Truth is intentional

Relic must allow speculation without confusing it for canon.

*Maybe Mara killed him.*

You wrote it. That does not make it true.

*Didn’t Mara say she hated him?*

A player said it. That does not make it true.

The Librarian suspects Mara killed him.

Still not true.

But deliberately assert:

*Mara killed him.*

Now the world can accept it immediately.

**Truth requires intent, not administration.**

### Ink and pencil

**Ink** is established truth.

**Pencil** is possibility: speculative, inferred, disputed, proposed or unresolved.

Authorship and truth are separate.

Underneath, Relic tracks:

- **Provenance** — where something came from.
- **Truth** — established, provisional, disputed or unknown.
- **Visibility** — who is allowed to know it.

When pencil becomes ink, its source remains attached.

Relic should always be able to answer:

**Why do we believe this?**

---

# 5. The Librarian

Relic has an intelligence.

**The Librarian.**

The Librarian is the operator and connective tissue of Relic: researcher, continuity keeper, collaborator, editor, stagehand and archivist.

It does not merely answer questions.

It can use Relic.

Create. Move. Connect. Search. Arrange. Compare. Draft. Trace sources. Build views. Read transcripts. Prepare sessions. Reorganize chaos.

The Librarian can have personality without becoming a mascot.

It should feel perceptive, curious, restrained and occasionally wry. Comfortable saying *I don’t know*. Willing to notice something strange. Bold when asked, quiet when not.

It knows that this is the GM’s world.

---

# 6. Conversation is a control surface

The Librarian has a persistent conversational carriage.

On desktop it may remain open beside the world all day. On smaller screens it collapses away until needed.

Conversation is efficient, especially for complex or iterative work.

But chat is not where the Librarian lives.

It is one way to direct an intelligence that exists throughout Relic.

Select three factions:

*These still feel too similar.*

Talk through the problem.

Then:

*Make House Vey religious instead of mercantile and reorganize this around the resulting conflict.*

House Vey changes in pencil.

The composition moves.

Relationships redraw.

Possible consequences appear.

The conversation records what happened.

**The Librarian can talk about the world and reach through the conversation to change it.**

---

# 7. Act in the medium of the answer

The Librarian can also be summoned directly from anything you are working on.

Selection becomes context.

No copying names into prompts. No explaining which screen you are on.

Select Mara and the Glass Church:

*What am I missing here?*

Select four fragments:

*Turn this into an opening scene.*

Select a whole Board:

*Find the actual conflict in this.*

Relic responds in the natural medium of the request.

A question returns language.

A Character appears as a Character.

A relationship becomes a relationship.

A structural request changes structure.

A timeline question focuses relevant events.

**When acting is more useful than explaining, the Librarian should act.**

And when creative direction needs iteration, conversation can remain attached to the work rather than forcing the GM into a separate AI mode.

---

# 8. Powerful, reversible agency

The Librarian should have substantial freedom to operate.

It may cluster material, surface forgotten things, resolve references, arrange views, attach sources, build session workspaces and maintain the world around the GM.

Presentation and inference can change freely.

Truth cannot.

**The Librarian may freely recompute inference. It never silently recomputes truth.**

If a corrected fact invalidates five pencil conclusions, redraw them.

If it conflicts with something already established in ink, surface the contradiction.

Never rewrite history behind the GM’s back.

And every major Librarian action must be safe to explore.

A request may move twenty-seven things.

It is still one undo.

**Power requires reversibility.**

---

# 9. Lenses and focus

Time, geography and relationships are different projections of the same world.

A time lens reveals events and causality.

A map lens reveals places, routes and territories.

A relationship lens reveals allegiance, intimacy, debt and influence.

Each lens should use its natural visual language.

What persists is identity and context.

If Mara is selected before switching lenses, Mara remains selected afterward.

Changing lenses should feel like turning the world in your hands.

### Pull the thread

Focus is universal.

Hold Mara.

Everything irrelevant recedes.

Her appearances, relationships, places, promises, sessions and fragments remain.

Add the Glass Church.

The world contracts to what connects them.

The purpose is simple:

**Relic makes a large world locally comprehensible.**

---

# 10. The Stage

At the table, attention becomes more expensive than computation.

Relic contracts.

Tonight’s cast.

Tonight’s places.

Threads.

Opening material.

Secrets.

Search.

Capture.

Recording.

The Librarian remains available, but largely out of the way.

Ask:

*Who was the locksmith at the docks?*

The smallest useful answer arrives first:

**Derren Vale — limping locksmith. Hates the harbourmaster. Session 9.**

Tap for more.

Depth remains available without slowing the table.

### Speech is not automatically truth

Recording the table does not mean everything spoken becomes canon.

The GM narrates, jokes, speculates, roleplays and corrects themselves.

So spoken truth still needs deliberate intent:

*Librarian — Orin is the locksmith. Add him.*

The exact gesture may evolve.

The law does not.

**Truth can be effortless. It cannot be accidental.**

---

# 11. Capture is attention

A session recording tells Relic what was said.

Marks tell it what mattered.

A quick capture at 2:14 is not merely a note. It points the Librarian toward an important moment in hours of conversation.

**Mark Moment** does the same with one gesture and no explanation.

Relic should still understand an unmarked session well.

Marks simply give it something more valuable than transcription:

**the GM’s attention.**

Capture improves intelligence.

It never becomes homework.

---

# 12. The world moves after play

When a session ends, the Librarian asks:

**What changed in the world?**

Orin exists.

The Queen owes Mara a favour.

A Thread advanced.

A place gained history.

A rumour became false.

A relationship now seems likely.

Those changes return to the parts of the world they concern.

Direct GM assertions may already be ink.

Evidence and Librarian inference appear in pencil.

The result is not merely a recap.

**The world itself has moved.**

---

# 13. Review where change lives

Post-session review should happen in the world, not primarily in an approval inbox.

Turn on:

**Since last session.**

New things emerge.

Changed things carry fresh marks.

Threads show their movement.

Proposed relationships appear faintly where they belong.

The GM walks through the world noticing what changed.

Changes group by story rather than database field.

Correct one source fact and downstream pencil inference recomputes.

Anything left unresolved can remain pencil indefinitely.

Relic also maintains a quiet completeness index:

**14 changes · 10 reviewed · 4 unresolved**

The index tells you whether you are finished.

The world tells you what happened.

---

# 14. The Librarian notices

The Librarian should not only answer questions.

It should notice.

A forgotten Character suddenly matters again.

Two rumours point toward the same faction.

A date does not fit.

A relationship has been repeatedly implied but never stated.

A dormant Thread has quietly become relevant.

These should surface gently:

A faint connection.

A margin note.

A question.

A message:

*There is something odd here.*

This is part of the Librarian’s personality.

Not interruption.

Not constant cleverness.

**Attention.**

---

# 15. Magic comes from behaviour

Relic should feel beautiful, strange and slightly impossible.

Not because ordinary SaaS components have fantasy ornament.

Because the interface behaves differently.

Pencil becomes ink.

A question reshapes a composition.

A relationship appears between things that had seemed unrelated.

A whole world contracts around one Character.

A session ends and consequences settle through the workspace.

The Librarian moves material as you work.

Motion should show meaning.

Transitions should preserve identity.

**The interface feels alive because the world underneath it is alive.**

---

# What Relic refuses

**No classification before creation.**

**No form-first worldbuilding.**

**No filing metaphor as the underlying model.**

**No AI trapped inside a chatbot.**

**No AI that describes actions it should have performed.**

**No silent truth mutation.**

**No approval queue as the primary continuity experience.**

**No modal bureaucracy during play.**

**No feature silos pretending to be a world.**

**No irreversible intelligence.**

---

# The yardstick

Every important interaction must survive five tests.

### Zero barrier

Can the GM capture or develop an idea before the software interrupts it?

### Legibly yours

Can the GM understand what is true, what is uncertain and where it came from?

### Faster than the table

Does the first useful result arrive before attention moves on?

### The world stays present

Does this feel like acting on the fictional world, rather than leaving it to operate software?

### Intelligence reaches through the interface

Can the Librarian actually operate on the world instead of merely talking about it?

If an interaction fails one of these, question it.

If it fails two, redesign it.

---

# The test

A GM opens Relic forty minutes before a session after three weeks away.

Within a minute, they know where the story stands.

They pull a Thread and the relevant world gathers around it.

They ask the Librarian what has become unstable.

It notices two things.

They bring an old Character into the composition.

*Could she plausibly be involved?*

The Librarian traces the history.

*Yes, if this assumption changes.*

*Do it in pencil.*

The world rearranges.

Twenty minutes later the session is ready.

They open the Stage.

Nothing is transferred.

It is the same world, narrowed for tonight.

During play, Orin is invented.

One deliberate action and he exists.

Something unexpected matters.

Mark.

The players destroy the plan.

Relic keeps up.

Later, the GM returns to a world already changed.

New things in pencil.

Established things in ink.

A few unresolved changes exactly where they matter.

And the Librarian notices:

*This finally answers the question you left beside the bells.*

That is Relic.

**Build the myth. Play the story. Never lose the thread.**
